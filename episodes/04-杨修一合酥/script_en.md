May, 219 AD. Hanzhong, China. Cao Cao's army is trapped in the mountains by Liu Bei. One day, Cao Cao gives a two-word order: "Chicken rib." His officers are baffled. Only one man is already packing. Yang Xiu, Cao Cao's secretary, the man who could always read his mind. He thought he'd just solved another riddle. But after this one, he had only months to live.

Yang Xiu's riddles began with a gate. On a new gate, Cao Cao wrote "alive." Yang Xiu had it rebuilt. "'Alive' inside 'gate' spells 'wide.'"

Someone sent Cao Cao a cup of cream. He wrote "together" on the lid. Yang Xiu ate. "Split it: one bite per person." A later novel made it a box of pastry.

Back to Hanzhong. A chicken rib: no meat, but a waste to throw away. That's Hanzhong, Yang Xiu said. In May, Cao Cao pulled out.

But the chicken rib didn't kill him. That's the novel. Yang Xiu had written a dozen answers in advance for Cao Cao's son, Cao Zhi. Every question, answered too fast. Cao Cao dug in, and it all came out. Cao Zhi was fighting his brother Cao Pi to be heir.

That autumn, Yang Xiu was executed, aged 45. He said: "I always knew I'd lived too long."

So what killed him? Being too clever, or picking the wrong side?
